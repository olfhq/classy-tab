From: kevins@ornews.intel.com (Kevin Smith) [email no longer active - May 2012]

Last week someone asked for the tab of the Bach Prelude BWV 999
(Praelude in C moll pour la Lute di J S Bach).  It is one of Bach's
most popular pieces for guitar.  It was originally in Cm, but here it
is transposed to Dm for guitar.  I like to play it brisk and flowing
with a slight accent on the base notes.  It is in 3/4 time; the |'s
represent 8th notes.  Let me know if you find any mistakes.

BWV 999 C-minor Lute Prelude: brought here to 1st position A-minor
tab by Jeff Kollasch, December 2023
MIDI: http://www.classtab.org/bach_js_bwv0999_prelude_in_cm_for_lute_am.mid

The standard version in D-minor is the best to perform in a concert by a pro.

This is in 1st position so it is easier. It is based on transposing the MIDI
for the prior version on ClassTab from D-minor down 5 half-steps to A-minor.

It is more arranged because bass notes that fall below the low E are moved up.
Specifically the Eb's (in this key) starting bars 11 and 12 are made an open E.
The very low C starting bar 15 is bumped up the octave. The very low B's that
start every bar from 17--32 are bumped up the octave. A 7-string guitar (low B)
would fit this arrangement perfectly with these notes back as they were. The
note shifts really do detract from the piece a little. I do not deny this.

It is 3/4 time and mostly all 16th notes as mentioned in the primary tab. The
official duration of the bass notes opening each bar is quarter notes, but
simply letting go of them when left-hand fingering requirements force you to
do so also works. I suspect this is what Bach intended for the lutenist.

LHF is only given where your first instincts are likely to betray you.

The best phrasing reference is Segovia. We are lucky he played this one on TV
in D-minor around 1953: https://youtu.be/s0lTdL3koak?si=JyR2x2UqmZqWAtp7

If you are like me and like to up-tune smaller or more sturdily built guitars
it is worthy of note that 2 half-steps up gets you to Bach's original key (C-
minor sounded ~1/2-step lower in 1700s, nearly the same as our B-minor today).
